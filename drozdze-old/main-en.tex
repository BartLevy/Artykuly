\abstract{The Apimondia position of 2025 rightly emphasizes that the honey production process is entirely natural and performed exclusively by bees. The article notes that the complex honey ripening process is closely linked to the decreasing water content during nectar processing by bees. The percentage of water in the product decreases as a result of honey being enriched with other substances contributed by the bee's organism, enzymatic processes, and the final ``active'' stage, which is the evaporation of water from honey deposited in combs. Honey stored in this way is capped by the bee in the comb, thereby protecting it from unfavorable changes resulting, for example, from the hygroscopic nature of the product. Although, as a rule, the production process itself ends at the stage of bees storing the processed nectar in combs, the product remains ``alive'' and variable in composition due to continuously occurring chemical, biochemical, and enzymatic processes. Under certain conditions (weather and/or climatic), the resulting product is susceptible to spoilage and requires human intervention. The authors draw attention to the fact that human involvement in the production process also encompasses beekeeping techniques as well as subsequent stages such as honey extraction, clarification, packaging, and storage. Human activity in this area should only be supportive, i.e., as non-invasive as possible with respect to the natural composition -- which should depend on the specificity of nectar sources and processes occurring naturally in the hive. At the interface between purely natural processes (occurring without human intervention) and those undertaken by humans, there appears a stage of verification and assessment of the justification and necessary scope of intervention. At this point, both good beekeeping practices and good honey packaging practices, based on technical equipment, regardless of their complexity, should be founded on specific, measurable parameters rather than on discretionary terms such as ``larger amounts,'' ``slight excess,'' ``insignificant excess,'' ``large amounts'' (\ldots). Assessment criteria should be based on risk evaluation. For this reason, this study describes in greater detail the relationship between water content and the risk of fermentation raised in the Apimondia position. It also contains specific proposals for assessing this phenomenon and research methods. The Apimondia position of 2025 identifies the production of immature
honey as a form of adulteration, making a significant contribution to the debate
on~product integrity. However, equating early harvest practices,
technological humidity regulation, and systemic production
of~immature honey oversimplifies a biologically and~technologically
complex continuum. This review article critically analyzes the concept
of honey maturity from the perspective of food microbiology,
metabolomics, and international regulatory standards.
We demonstrate that the presence of osmophilic yeasts, natural
and~ubiquitous components of honey, does not constitute a disqualifying
criterion in the absence of active fermentation, since microbiological
activity is determined by water activity ($a_w < 0{,}60$)
and temperature, not by the yeast cell count alone. No applicable
standard (Codex Alimentarius CODEX STAN 12-1981, EU Directive
2001/110/EC, ISO/DIS 24607:2025) establishes a CFU/g limit for yeasts
in~honey; product disqualification is based on evidence of fermentation,
not on the presence of microorganisms. We propose a \emph{functional
maturity} model integrating water activity measurement, enzymatic
activity profiling, and metabolomic biomarkers, which accounts for
the diversity of global beekeeping systems, including high-humidity
tropical environments. We argue that regulatory frameworks should
distinguish between technological honey stabilization and deliberate
adulteration, and that the mere presence of yeasts, in the absence of
active fermentation, should not constitute grounds for declaring
the product non-compliant with requirements.
}
