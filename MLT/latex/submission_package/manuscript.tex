\begin{abstract}

    This paper presents the development and validation of an objective, numerical measure of similarity between acoustic stimuli. The main aim of the study was to construct a metric based on a vector representation of sound features that would enable a quantitative assessment of the risk that samples composed of individual sounds are misidentified because of their acoustic proximity rather than because of listeners' cognitive deficits.

    The study material comprised 24 recordings of animal vocalizations, each described by a 16-dimensional feature vector comprising the spectral centroid, the fundamental frequency, the peak-to-average ratio, and 13 mel-frequency cepstral coefficients. Min-max normalization was applied to equalize the contribution of individual parameters, and the relationships between samples were analyzed using the Euclidean distance and principal component analysis.


    The key parameters differentiating sound timbre were identified, and no strong correlation was found between the spectral centroid and the fundamental frequency ($r = 0.26$), confirming that the individual descriptors carry non-redundant information. Comparison with the results of clinical testing showed preliminary agreement between the proposed metric and empirical perceptual difficulty: the stimulus pair with the smallest Euclidean distance ($d = 0.736$) was correctly identified in only $20.5\%$ of cases, whereas the more distant pairs reached $44.4$--$92.9\%$ correct responses, although the relationship was not monotonic. The proposed methodology provides a foundation for constructing reproducible stimulus sets with a controlled level of difficulty for auditory perception tests.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The need for comparative analyses of acoustic signals, including complex sound samples, stems from a range of research, diagnostic, and applied needs. One important area of application is audiological testing, in which the comparison of auditory stimuli with controlled physical parameters enables an objective assessment of the function of the auditory system and of the patient's health status.
Auditory stimuli that are similar in nature but carry different information remain ``legible'' when presented separately, whereas when they occur simultaneously they may become unintelligible owing to subtle similarities or small differences in their physical properties.

Comparative analysis of acoustic signals also deepens our understanding of the mechanisms of auditory perception, including the relationship between the physical properties of a stimulus and its subjective perception. In this context, methods that allow sound similarity to be described quantitatively are of particular importance, as they underpin research on stimulus discriminability, sensory adaptation, and the processing of acoustic information in the central nervous system.
The development of analytical tools that enable an objective and reproducible assessment of acoustic signal similarity is therefore an important element of interdisciplinary research combining acoustics, audiology, neurophysiology, and cognitive science.

One of the key challenges in research based on the analysis of acoustic stimuli is the large number of variables affecting the results, encompassing both the physical parameters of the signal and factors related to its recording conditions and to the individual perceptual characteristics of the listener~\cite{Letowski1989SoundQuality}. The variability of these parameters can be a significant methodological limitation, making unambiguous interpretation of the results difficult and reducing their reproducibility and reliability. In audiological testing and auditory perception research, it is particularly problematic to separate the influence of the properties of the stimulus itself from effects arising from its presentation or subjective interpretation.

Structured comparative methods based on measurable and unambiguously definable acoustic parameters make it possible to account for this variability systematically and to limit its influence on the final research conclusions. Representing sound samples as multidimensional feature vectors allows many aspects of the signal, such as spectral structure, amplitude dynamics, and cepstral characteristics, to be analyzed simultaneously, without having to resort each time to a complex and time-consuming physical assessment of individual parameters. This approach promotes the standardization of analytical procedures, increases the objectivity of comparisons, and enables a fast and reproducible assessment of acoustic signal similarity. As a result, vector analysis is an effective tool for interpreting research findings, allowing observed differences and similarities to be related more transparently to measurable properties of the auditory stimuli.

A number of methods exist for the detailed physical characterization of sound samples, based on the complementary use of time-domain, frequency-domain, and time--frequency analyses~\cite{Letowski1989SoundQuality,Peeters2004CUIDADO}. Time-domain analysis may include assessing the signal waveform over time by determining amplitude parameters (root-mean-square (RMS) value, dynamic range), the zero-crossing rate (ZCR) for a preliminary classification of periodic versus noise-like signals~\cite{Gouyon2000}, and the envelope characteristics (ADSR: attack, decay, sustain, release), which describe the temporal properties of the onset and decay of a sound~\cite{Peeters2004CUIDADO}. Frequency-domain analysis, in turn, uses the Fourier transform to determine the amplitude and phase spectra and to identify harmonic components, as well as the spectral centroid (timbral brightness), spectral flatness (tonality), and spectral roll-off (concentration of spectral energy)~\cite{Peeters2004CUIDADO,saitis2020brightness}.
For non-stationary signals, the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) and spectrograms are used to track spectral changes over time, complemented by psychoacoustically motivated descriptors such as mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) and perceptual linear prediction (PLP) coefficients, which model the nonlinearity of human hearing by means of mel-scale and critical-band filter banks~\cite{Hermansky1990,tracey2023interpretable_mfcc}.
Such a comprehensive analytical approach enables an objective assessment of the physical and perceptual properties of given sound samples, which may form the basis for further analyses of their similarity~\cite{Peeters2004CUIDADO}.

Comparing sound samples on the basis of their physical components, i.e., the physical properties of the auditory stimulus, and attempting to determine whether specific components of different stimuli are responsible for difficulties in understanding the information conveyed by each sample, is a considerable challenge owing to the complexity of the analytical processes involved. Hence, there is a need for simpler, parametric, and more transparent algorithms for analyzing the degree of similarity and/or difference between the auditory stimuli being assessed.

The present study addresses the selection of physical features of acoustic stimuli, followed by the assignment and computation of comparable vector quantities for the individual physical features of sound samples, in order to test the research hypothesis that the Euclidean distance between normalized vectors of selected acoustic features of sound samples is an objective measure of the similarity (or dissimilarity) of the compared auditory stimuli~\cite{MadhavanWrobel2024MLT} and that it correlates with how well these stimuli are discriminated in clinical tests. Specifically, the authors sought to verify the assumption that the misidentification of a pair of auditory stimuli played during clinical testing may be caused by a ``small'' distance between them in the Euclidean space of features previously selected and defined for the purposes of this analysis, as opposed to the correct identification of pairs separated by a ``large'' Euclidean distance. The results of the Memory Load Test (MLT) study conducted by Madhavan et al.~\cite{MadhavanWrobel2024MLT}, in which participants were asked to identify the number of simultaneously presented animal sounds, serve as the reference for this verification.


\section{Materials and methods}
\label{section:mat_met}

The main premise of the analysis was to develop a specific measure for complex auditory stimuli that would allow a mathematical assessment of how similar individual signals (auditory stimuli) are to one another. In other words, the aim of the study was (Part A) to develop an acoustic similarity metric based on distances between normalized acoustic feature vectors of sound samples, and subsequently (Part B) to verify the resulting metric against the results of clinical experiments on the perception of complex auditory stimuli.

The study material consisted of sound samples representing the vocalizations of common animals, prepared as MP3 files (MPEG-1 Audio Layer III). All 24 files have a sampling rate of 44.1~kHz. Three of them (\textit{cat.mp3}, \textit{gorilla.mp3}, and \textit{hawk.mp3}) were resampled to 44.1~kHz from originals sampled at 11.025~kHz (\textit{cat.mp3}, \textit{gorilla.mp3}) and 22.05~kHz (\textit{hawk.mp3}); their effective bandwidth is therefore limited to approximately 5~kHz and 11~kHz, respectively. Three recordings from the MLT set (\textit{elephant.mp3}, \textit{frog.mp3}, and \textit{horse.mp3}) were originally stored in AAC format and were converted to MP3. Twenty-one recordings were stereo and 3 (\textit{cat.mp3}, \textit{gorilla.mp3}, and \textit{hawk.mp3}) were mono. Features were extracted at the native sampling rate of each file after downmixing to a single (mono) channel. The recordings varied in duration from 3.2~s (\textit{hawk.mp3}) to 162.3~s (\textit{duck.mp3}), with a mean of 32.9~s and a median of 9.1~s; the total size of the set was approximately 13.7~MB.

In total, 24 sound files representing different animal species were prepared. The first part comprised 10 recordings used as stimulus material in the MLT study conducted by Madhavan et al.~\cite{MadhavanWrobel2024MLT}: chicken.mp3, cow.mp3, dog.mp3, donkey.mp3, elephant.mp3, frog.mp3, horse.mp3, lion.mp3, monkey.mp3, and sheep.mp3. As the original stimulus set of that study, these recordings are hereafter referred to as the \textit{MLT set}. They were supplemented with 14 animal recordings selected at random from the BBC Sound Effects library~\cite{bbcrewind_soundeffects}: blackbird.mp3, chaffinch.mp3, great\_tit.mp3, tundra\_swan.mp3, wolf1.mp3, cat.mp3, duck.mp3, frog2.mp3, gorilla.mp3, hawk.mp3, owl.mp3, tawny\_owl.mp3, wild\_boar.mp3, and wolf2.mp3. Together, the 24 recordings form the \textit{extended set}.

The feature set was chosen according to the principle of a parsimonious yet complementary description of the signal, so as to cover three relatively independent perceptual--acoustic domains while keeping each dimension interpretable. Timbre was described by the spectral centroid (the best-established correlate of perceived timbral ``brightness''~\cite{Grey1977Timbre,Lakatos2000Timbre,saitis2020brightness}) and by a set of 13 MFCCs, which provide a standard, compact description of the shape of the spectral envelope~\cite{Peeters2011TimbreToolbox,Siedenburg2017TimbreSimilarity,DeCamargo2017ProtaxSound}; their number follows common convention and the default configuration of the \textit{librosa} library~\cite{mcfee2015librosa}. Pitch was represented by the fundamental frequency (F0), whose independence from timbre is supported by its weak correlation with the spectral centroid ($r = 0.26$). Temporal dynamics were captured by the peak-to-average ratio (PAR), which makes it possible to distinguish signals of similar pitch and timbre but different impulsive character. This choice was further supported by practical considerations: the unambiguous interpretation of each feature (important when the metric is used to audit stimulus material), a small vector dimension appropriate to the size of the dataset (24~recordings), the availability of all descriptors in standard open-source extraction tools, and consistency with the representation used by Madhavan et al.~\cite{MadhavanWrobel2024MLT}, whose work the present study extends. A deliberately simple and interpretable baseline representation was therefore adopted, serving as a reference point for richer feature sets.

Because of substantial differences in the units and scales of the measured parameters (e.g., frequencies in Hz reaching values of several thousand, compared with MFCCs of much smaller magnitude), and in order to prevent parameters with high absolute values (such as the spectral centroid) from dominating the remaining features in statistical computations, all extracted features were transformed to a common range of $[0, 1]$ using the \textit{MinMaxScaler} class of the \textit{scikit-learn} library, version 1.7.2~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}.
The acoustic feature vector of each sound sample was formed by concatenating the time-averaged values of each computed feature, 16 features in total.


Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the multidimensional acoustic feature set and to enable its clear visualization~\cite{salimi2025evaluating,liu2023voiceprint}. The starting point was a data matrix in which each sound sample was described by a feature vector comprising spectral and cepstral parameters, including the MFCCs and selected energy measures.
Before the analysis proper, the data were centered and standardized to eliminate the effect of scale differences between individual features. The covariance matrix was then computed and used to determine the eigenvalues and eigenvectors defining new, orthogonal axes of data variability. The eigenvectors were sorted in descending order of their corresponding eigenvalues, and the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which explain the largest share of the total variance of the dataset, were retained for further analysis. Finally, the sound samples were projected onto the plane spanned by PC1 and PC2, which made it possible to display them on a two-dimensional plot and to further analyze similarities and distances between the signals in the reduced feature space.

Ultimately, combining these two dimensions reduces the complex 16-dimensional audio model to a single, easily interpretable point on the plane, providing insight into the objective, clinically relevant similarities between the examined samples.

The feature vectors of the audio files were compared using the Euclidean distance (the shortest, straight-line distance between two points in the feature space), which in sound analysis allows the degree of similarity between signals to be determined mathematically.

\subsubsection{Spectral centroid}

The spectral centroid ranged from $5257.504$~Hz (\textit{tawny\_owl.mp3}) to $1282.841$~Hz (\textit{cat.mp3}). The mean and median for the analyzed group were $3007.300$~Hz and $2971.778$~Hz, respectively, with a standard deviation of $1152.948$~Hz. Most samples are concentrated in the lower brightness range, as confirmed by the normalized median ($\text{med} = 0.425$). The sample \textit{tawny\_owl.mp3} ($c = 5257.504$~Hz) raises the mean centroid value and stands apart from the rest of the set.
The next highest value in the set (\textit{blackbird.mp3}, $c = 4619.491$~Hz) is $638.013$~Hz lower. This is the largest gap between consecutive centroid values in the whole set (all other gaps between adjacent values are below $365$~Hz), which sets this recording apart as a case of particularly high spectral brightness. \subsubsection{PAR (peak-to-average ratio)}
PAR ranged from $27.325$~dB (for the file with the highest dynamics, \textit{duck.mp3}) to $10.051$~dB (\textit{dog.mp3}); the mean and median for the analyzed group were $18.180$~dB and $18.047$~dB, respectively, with a standard deviation of $3.748$~dB.

The median ($0.463$) is very close to the mean, suggesting a balanced distribution of the crest factor across the whole set of 24 samples, without extreme anomalies. The first coefficient (MFCC\_1), which reflects the overall spectral energy, ranged from $-189.880$ (\textit{sheep.mp3}) to $-483.287$ (\textit{frog.mp3}), with a mean of $-316.674$. Such a large standard deviation ($85.493$) indicates that the samples differ from one another mainly in loudness and overall signal power.

The spectral characteristics are dominated by MFCC\_2 ($\text{avg} = 109.258$) and MFCC\_3 ($\text{avg} = -38.894$), which carry the most information about the ``warmth'' or ``sharpness'' of the sound. The predominance of MFCC\_2 suggests strongly defined lower frequency bands. In contrast, the values of the remaining coefficients (MFCC\_4--MFCC\_13) lie closer to zero (from $\text{avg} = -9.328$ to $\text{avg} = 3.572$), indicating a smoothing of the higher timbral components that is typical of natural animal vocalizations.

Normalization allowed all 13 coefficients to be compared directly and showed that the set is well balanced. More than half of the coefficients (7 of 13) have mean values in the range $0.45$--$0.55$, and none exceeds $0.62$, which means that the set is statistically homogeneous, with no dominant parameter that could mask the remaining features. MFCC\_8 has the highest normalized mean ($\text{avg} = 0.618$) and, at the same time, the largest standard deviation among the MFCCs ($\text{std} = 0.292$), indicating that the samples differ most strongly in this band. MFCC\_2, in turn, shows the greatest stability: its standard deviation, the lowest among the MFCCs ($\text{std} = 0.223$), makes it a common denominator for all 24 recordings. MFCC\_9 also has a relatively high standard deviation ($\text{std} = 0.278$), indicating that the information carried by this coefficient has a notable effect on the differentiation of the sound samples. Correlation analysis was used as a test of feature redundancy: if two variables are strongly correlated, then in a metric based on the Euclidean distance they may in practice duplicate information about a sound sample and amplify one dimension of difference at the expense of another. Low correlation means that the features describe different aspects of the signal and that their joint use improves the discrimination of acoustic similarity (e.g., the independence of the centroid from F0 means that timbre/brightness does not follow directly from pitch).

\subsubsection{Correlation analysis of audio parameters}
The correlation matrix is a tabular summary of the correlation coefficients computed for each pair of analyzed parameters, describing the strength and direction of the linear relationship between them. The coefficient takes values from $-1$ to $+1$: values close to $+1$ indicate a strong positive relationship (an increase in one feature co-occurs with an increase in the other), values close to $-1$ indicate a strong negative relationship (an increase in one feature co-occurs with a decrease in the other), and values close to zero indicate the absence of a linear relationship, i.e., the features vary independently of each other in the linear sense. In the context of the present study, the analysis of the correlation matrix serves as a redundancy test: strongly correlated features carry largely the same information and, in a metric based on the Euclidean distance, may artificially amplify one dimension of difference at the expense of the others, whereas weakly correlated features describe different aspects of the signal, and taking them into account jointly increases the ability to discriminate acoustic similarity. Identifying these relationships therefore makes it possible to assess whether the adopted set of descriptors is mutually complementary.


Three non-MFCC descriptors, namely the spectral centroid, F0, and PAR, supplemented by three representative MFCCs, were selected for the correlation analysis. Including the centroid, F0, and PAR was essential, because these parameters represent three distinct domains of the feature vector (spectral brightness, pitch, and temporal dynamics, respectively); placing them in a single matrix makes it possible to verify whether they indeed carry mutually independent information, which constitutes a direct test of feature redundancy in a metric based on the Euclidean distance. Of the MFCCs, MFCC\_1 (signal energy) and MFCC\_2 (global timbral component and spectral tilt) were selected because they reach the highest absolute values in the MFCC space ($-316.674$ and $109.258$, respectively), together with MFCC\_9 as an example of a higher-order coefficient with high variability in the examined set (standard deviation after normalization $\text{std} = 0.278$). The presentation was limited to this subset to keep the matrix legible and to highlight the relationships between the main, interpretable acoustic parameters. This restriction, however, applies only to the illustration: in the PCA, the principal components (PC1 and PC2) are computed from the full feature vector (centroid, F0, PAR, and MFCC\_1--MFCC\_13), so the comparison of sound samples takes into account the combined information carried by all descriptors, not only the selected coefficients.     In the examined dataset, the relationship between the spectral centroid and the fundamental frequency F0 is weak (correlation coefficient $0.26$). \section{Discussion}

The question of how to compare two sounds is deceptively simple. In practice, acoustic similarity and perceptual similarity are multidimensional constructs and cannot be reliably described by a single signal parameter. Classic studies of timbre already showed that similarity judgments rely simultaneously on several partially independent spectral and temporal features. Grey applied multidimensional scaling to similarity ratings of instrument timbres, while Lakatos showed that the main perceptual dimensions include, among others, the spectral centroid and the attack (rise) time~\cite{Grey1977Timbre,Lakatos2000Timbre}. Later studies confirmed that the perceptual timbre space can be described by a combination of several acoustic features rather than by a single variable~\cite{Elliott2013TimbreDimensions}.

With regard to environmental sounds, the study by Gygi et al., which analyzed 50 natural sounds, is particularly relevant. The authors showed that similarity judgments form a multidimensional structure and that the position of stimuli in this space can be partly predicted from a combination of features such as harmonicity, the proportion of silence, and modulation depth~\cite{Gygi2007EnvironmentalSounds}. This finding is directly relevant to the present work, because the sound material analyzed here also consists of complex, semantically recognizable sounds rather than laboratory pure tones. It follows that the similarity between such stimuli should be treated as the outcome of the interplay of many signal properties rather than as a function of pitch alone or of a single spectral measure.

Modern sound analysis methods make it possible to reverse the classic paradigm of psychophysical research: instead of first asking listeners which sounds are similar, one can first build a numerical representation of each stimulus and then compute the distance between these representations. The development of descriptor sets such as the \textit{Timbre Toolbox} (a toolbox for the automatic description of timbre and other sound properties by means of multiple acoustic parameters) has made it possible to capture signal structure through numerous spectral, energy, and temporal parameters~\cite{Peeters2011TimbreToolbox}. MFCCs, in turn, have become the standard means of compactly describing the shape of the spectral envelope and are widely used in speech and music analysis, source identification, and sound classification. It should be emphasized that neither the use of MFCCs nor the use of the Euclidean distance constitutes a methodological novelty in itself. Helén and Virtanen already used distance measures between feature representations in an \textit{audio query-by-example} system, comparing, among others, the Euclidean distance with measures based on probability distributions~\cite{HelenVirtanen2010AudioQuery}. De Camargo et al. applied MFCCs and distance measures to the automatic identification of animal sounds~\cite{DeCamargo2017ProtaxSound}, while Siedenburg and Müllensiefen modeled explicit similarity ratings of short musical excerpts using sets of timbre descriptors and MFCCs, with inter-feature distances as predictors of listeners' ratings~\cite{Siedenburg2017TimbreSimilarity}. These studies confirm that a vector representation of the signal is a well-founded way of quantifying similarity, but they also delineate the boundary beyond which the actual novelty of the present approach must be sought.

The distinctiveness of the present work therefore lies neither in proposing a new class of descriptors nor in a new mathematical definition of distance, but in assigning this distance a different experimental role. In typical sound recognition or retrieval systems, the distance between vectors serves as a classification criterion: the output is meant to indicate what the sound is, or which item in a database is most similar to the query sound. In psychophysical research, in turn, distance in feature space is often related to explicit similarity judgments made by the listener. In the present study, by contrast, acoustic distance is treated as an independent property of stimulus design and as a potential factor affecting performance in a perceptual--cognitive task. This is important because the participants in the original study described by Madhavan et al.~\cite{MadhavanWrobel2024MLT} were not asked whether two sounds were similar. Their task was to identify the number of simultaneously presented auditory objects. Acoustic similarity was thus not the direct subject of their responses, but it may have acted as a hidden factor hindering auditory scene segregation. From this perspective, the question ``how to compare two sounds?'' has methodological significance: it concerns not only describing their similarity but also assessing whether a pair of stimuli is acoustically close enough to alter the difficulty of the test independently of the cognitive processes the test is intended to measure.

Such use of the metric has practical implications for the design of stimulus material in audiological and cognitive research. Two stimuli may belong to different semantic categories and yet remain close in the multidimensional feature space. When presented simultaneously, they may be difficult to separate because of similarities in spectral structure, pitch, dynamics, or other signal properties. Consequently, the test outcome may reflect both the cognitive ability under study and the properties of the sound material itself. An objective analysis of inter-stimulus distances can therefore serve as quality control for a test set and help identify overly similar pairs before a clinical study begins.

The results obtained provide only partial and preliminary support for this interpretation. Of the four pairs for which data from the study by Madhavan et al.~\cite{MadhavanWrobel2024MLT} were available, the pair \textit{cow.mp3--horse.mp3}, which had the smallest distance ($d = 0.736$), was also the pair with by far the lowest percentage of correct responses ($20.5\%$), and each of the three pairs more distant in feature space was identified correctly much more often. This is consistent with the hypothesis that the acoustic proximity of stimuli may increase the load on auditory segregation processes. Beyond this distance, however, the distance value did not order task difficulty: \textit{cow.mp3--lion.mp3} ($d = 0.929$) yielded $92.9\%$ correct responses, \textit{donkey.mp3--horse.mp3} ($d = 1.102$) $55.6\%$, and \textit{sheep.mp3--frog.mp3}, despite the largest Euclidean distance ($d = 1.710$), only $44.4\%$. This pattern may point to a threshold effect, in which a very small acoustic distance markedly increases task difficulty, rather than to a linear difficulty gradient; with only four pairs, however, it is not possible to decide between these interpretations, and conclusions must therefore remain cautious. These results further show that a single global distance measure cannot be treated as a complete predictor of perceptual difficulty. Task performance may also be affected by the temporal properties of the signal, the onset synchrony of the stimuli, amplitude and frequency modulation, differences in duration, semantic features and source recognizability, as well as attentional mechanisms and auditory scene organization. For this reason, the present data should be regarded as a pilot proof-of-concept validation rather than as the establishment of a universal similarity threshold.

The interpretation of the extended set of 24 stimuli also warrants clarification. The minimum distance in the MLT set was $0.736$, for the pair \textit{cow.mp3--horse.mp3}, whereas an even closer pair, \textit{owl.mp3--tawny\_owl.mp3} ($d = 0.500$), was found in the extended set. This means that extending the set did in fact introduce a pair with greater acoustic similarity, rather than merely broadening the coverage of the feature space without increasing redundancy, as one might mistakenly conclude. From the perspective of the aim of the study, this result shows that the proposed procedure can detect potentially problematic stimulus pairs before they are used in a perceptual study.

\subsection{Importance of feature selection and interpretive limitations}

The combination of the spectral centroid, F0, PAR, and MFCCs yields a relatively simple and interpretable vector covering different aspects of the signal. The advantage of this solution is that a complex acoustic description can be reduced to a single distance measure while retaining information about many features simultaneously. The results of the correlation analysis indicate that some parameters contribute relatively distinct information; for example, the relationship between F0 and the spectral centroid was weak. This argues for combining descriptors rather than reducing similarity to a single dimension. However, an overly literal interpretation of individual MFCCs should be avoided. These coefficients describe successive components of the spectral envelope on the mel scale, with increasingly fine details of the spectral shape represented as the coefficient index increases~\cite{Siedenburg2017TimbreSimilarity}. There is no universal mapping such as ``MFCC\_5 = a specific resonance'' or ``MFCC\_9 = microstructure'' that is independent of the material and the extraction parameters. For the same reason, a high mean value of a normalized MFCC does not imply high discriminative power of that feature. Assessing the actual importance of individual dimensions would require, for example, feature importance analysis, cross-validation of models with successive parameters removed, or the application of dimensionality reduction and regression methods to a larger dataset.

A second limitation concerns the way information is aggregated over time. In the current procedure, feature values are averaged over the entire recording. This approach is simple and stable, but it discards information about signal dynamics: the onset, envelope, modulation, spectral changes, and the order of events. Lakatos demonstrated the importance of rise time for timbre perception~\cite{Lakatos2000Timbre}, and the work of Siedenburg and Müllensiefen shows that spectrotemporal features and MFCC derivatives can contribute important information when modeling similarity~\cite{Siedenburg2017TimbreSimilarity}. In future work, it would therefore be worthwhile to compare the current model with a representation that includes delta-MFCCs, temporal envelope parameters, or sequence alignment methods such as \textit{dynamic time warping}.

In this project, the Euclidean distance has an important advantage: it is simple, unambiguous, and easy to interpret. After feature normalization, each parameter contributes to the distance on a comparable scale, which yields a transparent global measure. At the same time, this choice implies the assumption that all dimensions are equally important and that the feature space is geometrically isotropic. This assumption need not reflect the actual perceptual weight of individual parameters.

The literature on audio retrieval and similarity modeling indicates that the effectiveness of a given metric depends on the type of material and the purpose of the analysis~\cite{HelenVirtanen2010AudioQuery,Siedenburg2017TimbreSimilarity}. The next stage of research should therefore compare the Euclidean distance with other measures, e.g., the cosine distance, the Mahalanobis distance, or metrics learned from behavioral data. It would be particularly interesting to derive feature weights from a larger set of responses given by participants in the MLT study.
%, instead of assuming an equal contribution of each dimension.
The present model should therefore be regarded as a deliberately simple and interpretable baseline metric, serving as a reference point for more complex solutions.

The most practical application of the presented procedure is the possibility of pre-standardizing sound material. In tests designed to assess cognitive, memory, or perceptual integration processes, stimuli should be selected so that unintended acoustic proximity does not become the dominant source of difficulty. Computing a distance matrix for the entire set makes it possible to identify particularly close pairs, control the distribution of difficulty, and build alternative sets with a similar acoustic profile. This is potentially useful not only for assessing the subitizing effect but also for other audiological procedures and auditory perception experiments. At the same time, the present study is exploratory. The number of stimuli is small, and the behavioral validation covers only four pairs. On this basis, reliable thresholds classifying sounds as ``similar,'' ``intermediate,'' or ``different'' cannot be established. Future studies will require the validation of a larger number of pairs, the analysis of trials involving more than two simultaneous stimuli, and a direct comparison of the acoustic measure with both explicit similarity judgments and task performance in the MLT study. Such an approach would make it possible to separate three levels: the physical similarity of the signals, consciously reported perceptual similarity, and the actual effect of similarity on task performance. In this context, the proposed method should not be presented as a universal way of deciding whether two sounds are ``the same'' or ``different.'' Rather, its value lies in providing an objective, reproducible, and easily auditable index of acoustic proximity that can be used as an additional variable in the design and interpretation of auditory tests. This perspective links signal analysis with psychophysics and clinical application, moving from the question of which features a sound contains to the question of the extent to which stimulus properties can co-determine listener behavior.

In summary, previous research clearly shows that the quantitative comparison of sounds using multidimensional representations and distance measures rests on well-established methodological foundations. The novelty of the proposed approach therefore lies not in the signal analysis tools themselves but in their application to the control of stimulus material and to the interpretation of perceptual task performance. The fact that the pair with the smallest acoustic distance was identified correctly least often suggests that a multi-feature metric can help identify stimuli that carry an additional risk of interference. At the same time, the lack of a monotonic relationship among the remaining pairs and the small number of available comparisons indicate that this relationship requires further prospective validation. Seen in this light, the presented procedure is not a final model of sound perception but a practical and transparent tool for addressing a fundamental question: how can two sounds be compared in a way that is relevant to listener behavior?
